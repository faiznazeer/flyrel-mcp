# Community research: actual use of AI in investing

Prepared October 29, 2026; customer-discovery scope revised October 30, 2026.
These are drafts for the owner to adapt and post manually, not messages already sent or permission
to contact anyone. No community research responses have been recorded in this project.
See the [product hypothesis](product-direction.md). The aim is to discover barriers, including
barriers that would make an independent trading-permissions service unnecessary or undesirable.

The customer segment is undecided. Companies building trading agents, brokers, quant firms if
relevant needs emerge, developers and individual/multi-account traders are candidate audiences,
without ranking. Existing retail-oriented Reddit drafts remain exploratory; they do not establish
that retail is the target market. Company users, integration owners, security/risk approvers and
budget holders may be different people and require separate discovery.

## Research approach

Ask about actual behavior before opinions about a solution. Do not lead with security, a failure
percentage, a guardrail feature list, guaranteed safety, or "would you use a safer agent?".
Disclose the author's product-building interest without pitching the security hypothesis first.
Avoid a signup link or product demo in the initial question.

Use one short post suited to the community. Invite current, former and non-users. Ask about a
recent task, what happened next, what they kept doing elsewhere and why. A decision to keep
execution manual is a valid outcome, not an objection to overcome. Distinguish use of an LLM to
write code from use of an LLM to make decisions or initiate broker actions.

Only discuss a permissions/approval concept after the participant has given their unprompted
account, ideally in a separate consented follow-up. Label responses after the concept as prompted.
An open-ended post is still influenced by its wording and audience; unsolicited comments are
not an unbiased survey of all investors or evidence of institutional purchasing demand.

Method references consulted October 29, 2026:

- [Pew: writing survey questions](https://www.pewresearch.org/writing-survey-questions/): wording,
  answer choices and question order can influence which concerns people report. Open questions
  can surface topics a proposed list omits.
- [GOV.UK: in-depth interviews](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/using-in-depth-interviews):
  use neutral questions and concrete recent experiences, with consent and secure handling of notes.

## Draft A — individual investors, current and non-users

**Title:** Do you use ChatGPT or Claude for investing? What has actually stayed in your routine?

I'm curious about what people actually do with these tools once the initial experimenting wears off.

If you use one, what was the last investing-related task you gave it? What did you do with the
answer, and would you use it for that task again?

If you tried it and stopped, what made you stop? If you've never used one for investing, what's
the main reason you haven't tried it?

I'm interested in where it fits into your existing process, including anything you still prefer
to do another way and why.

Disclosure: I'm building an early open-source investing tool and researching existing workflows.
General examples are enough; please leave out account identifiers and private financial details.

## Draft B — general-purpose assistant users

Replace `[assistant]` with ChatGPT or Claude, matching the community before posting.

**Title:** Anyone using [assistant] regularly for investing? What do you actually use it for?

Has [assistant] become part of your investing or trading routine, or was it something you tried
and moved on from?

I'd like to hear about the last task you used it for: what you asked, what happened next, and
whether it saved work or created more work. What do you still do outside the assistant, and why?

If you don't use it for investing at all, what is the main reason?

Disclosure: I'm building an early open-source tool in this area and trying to understand actual
workflows. Please keep examples general and leave out private account details.

## Draft C — experienced systematic traders

**Title:** What uses of LLMs have survived in your trading workflow after the initial experiments?

For people who've tried general-purpose LLMs alongside a trading workflow, what are you still
using them for today, and what did you drop?

A recent example would help: what task did the model handle, what happened next, and what still
needed work elsewhere? If you abandoned an approach, what specifically made it not worth keeping?

If you haven't found a useful role for them, I'd be interested in that reasoning too.

Disclosure: I'm building an early open-source investing tool and researching existing workflows.
I'm asking about the process; there's no need to share a strategy, code, positions or account details.

## Draft D — people who stopped using AI

Use only in a community/thread where the topic and research are permitted. This deliberately
samples abandoned trials; it cannot estimate how common abandonment is.

**Title:** Tried AI for investing or trading and stopped? What happened?

What were you trying to get done, which tool did you try, and what happened on the attempt that
made you stop? What do you use for that task now?

I'm interested in what was missing and how much work the alternative takes. If nothing would
make you want to use AI for that task, that is useful to hear too.

Disclosure: I'm building an early open-source investing tool and doing early workflow research.
Please keep examples general and leave out private financial or account information.

## Neutral follow-up replies

Use one at a time, following what the person actually said:

- "Could you walk me through the most recent time that happened?"
- "What did you do next?"
- "Was that something you experienced yourself, or something you expected might happen?"
- "What did you use instead, and what made that preferable?"
- "How often does that task come up? Roughly how much time does it take?"
- "What, if anything, would have changed your choice on that occasion?"
- "Have you already tried a workaround? How did it go?"

If security/control comes up unprompted, reuse their wording. Ask which action or information
was involved and what they did about it. Do not turn the reply into a feature pitch. Distinguish
unwanted execution, unreliable analysis, investment loss, credential access, privacy and distrust
of another intermediary; they are different problems.

## Organizational discovery — customer not yet selected

Use this as a separate, consented conversation with a company building trading agents, a broker,
or a quant team if they have a relevant workflow. Do not assume that an organization uses LLMs
for trading or that its controls are inadequate. A person experimenting privately is not evidence
of their employer's deployment or demand. These are optional questions for manual research, not
authorization for an agent to contact organizations.

Suggested introduction:

> I'm exploring an early open-source project around agentic trading and trying to understand
> existing workflows before choosing a customer segment. Where, if anywhere, do agents fit into
> systems you work on? A high-level example is enough; please leave out proprietary strategies,
> client information and confidential system details.

Start with their experience and ask one follow-up at a time:

- "Can you describe the last relevant project or task, including one you decided not to pursue?"
- "What ran in an experiment, what reached regular use, and what was left outside the agent?"
- "What was hardest to make work, and what did the team do about it?"
- "How often does that problem occur? What existing tools or internal work does it require?"
- "Who uses the system day to day, and who is responsible for its operating decisions?"
- "How does the team decide whether to build a component internally or use a supplier?"
- "Who would evaluate an integration and who, if anyone, would approve spending on it?"
- "What would rule out a third-party component for this workflow?"

Only after hearing the unprompted problem, ask about the enforcement/authorization concept below.
If there is a concrete fit, explore required deployment boundaries, ownership, interfaces,
evaluation evidence and operational responsibilities using the participant's own examples.
Do not substitute a feature wish list for an actual buying process or claim that institutional
demand has been validated by technical interest. No employer names or confidential architecture
are needed in committed research summaries.

## Later concept test — separate from open discovery

After permission for a follow-up, a balanced concept description could be:

> I'm exploring a service between an assistant and a broker that would check exact order requests
> against permissions set outside the assistant and require a separate human approval before a
> permitted order could be submitted. It would add setup and approval steps, and another service
> to trust. It would not guarantee profits or eliminate software, broker or market risks. This
> independent enforcement and external execution are not implemented in the current prototype.

This describes the initial per-order approval experiment. The eventual interface, deployment
and approval model are undecided; organizational requirements need their own design. An agent
must not gain authority to grant or weaken its own permissions in any proposed model.

Then ask:

- "For the task you described, what would this help with, if anything?"
- "What would it leave unresolved or make worse?"
- "How would you decide whether to trust that service?"
- "Would you still prefer your current process? Why?"

Ask about a concrete, optional prototype session only after hearing the response. Do not present
hypothetical willingness to use/pay as observed adoption. Broker-paper or live execution requires
a separate authorized milestone; no research session should require a real-money trade.

## Record and interpret evidence

Keep raw notes and respondent identifiers out of version control. Use anonymized labels, obtain
agreement before private follow-up or retaining identifiable quotations, and do not collect
credentials, balances, positions, account screenshots or broker login details. No automatic
scraping or participant contact is part of this plan.

Suggested private note fields:

```text
Anonymous participant label / date / audience source:
Current, former or non-user; personal or professional context:
Candidate segment and participant role (operator, developer, integration/security/risk owner, buyer):
Whose experience/decision does the answer represent? individual / team / authorized company view:
Actual recent task; general-purpose assistant and role in the task:
What happened next / retained or abandoned / alternative workflow:
Main reason in the participant's words (anonymized):
Observed event or anticipated concern:
Was the issue mentioned before any concept or safeguard question? yes/no:
Recurring frequency, time/cost and attempted workaround, if volunteered:
Other explanations or counterexamples:
Existing tools/internal work; evaluation, build-versus-buy and purchasing process, if discussed:
Concept shown? yes/no; response and additional friction (prompted evidence):
Optional follow-up agreed? yes/no (contact details stored separately):
```

After open responses, derive themes from the answers rather than forcing a security category.
Possible analyst-only labels include usefulness, information quality, data availability,
setup effort, ongoing cost, latency, control/execution mistakes, credentials/privacy, preference
for manual decisions, and trust in intermediaries. Add new labels when needed and allow multiple
labels. Never use this list as the initial participant answer choices.

Report unprompted and prompted themes separately, with anonymous participant counts and audience
context. Separate personal feedback from organizational workflow and purchasing evidence. Do not
combine platform communities, developer samples, company roles and individual investors into an
adoption percentage. Likes, hypothetical interest, duplicate commenters and an owner walkthrough
are not independent paying customers. Stronger signals include repeated concrete tasks, existing
workarounds and a voluntary return for another bounded prototype session.

Revisit the hypothesis if the recurring problem is poor answers, missing data, insufficient
benefit, or a preference to keep execution manual regardless of controls. Also test whether people
would trust a broker-native control more than Flyrel. Preserve disconfirming evidence alongside
supportive feedback; see the decision criteria in [product direction](product-direction.md).
